1 · Standard flow shop: buffers allowed

J1 finishes on M1 at t = 3, but M2 is busy with J2 until t = 4. J1 waits 1 unit in the buffer. The same thing happens before M3 (t = 7 → 8). The machines stay busy, so Cmax = 10.

2 · No-wait flow shop: a started job never stops

M1 is free at t = 1, but J1 cannot start then: it would arrive at M2 while J2 is still there. J1 is held for 2 units and starts at t = 3. All transitions have zero gap (vertical links). The machines absorb the delay as idle time, so Cmax = 12.

What the no-wait rule changes

  • The wait does not go away. It moves. In the standard shop, J1 waits 2 units in total, in the buffers between machines. In the no-wait shop, J1 waits the same 2 units, but before it enters M1.
  • The makespan increases from 10 to 12 (+20%). A held job delays every job behind it. J3 also starts 2 units later on M1, and the line finishes later.
  • Utilisation decreases on all machines. M1: 70% → 58%. M2: 80% → 67%. M3: 60% → 50%. The work stays the same, but the machines stand idle while the next job is held.
  • Machine idle time replaces buffer time. M2 is idle 4 → 5 and 8 → 9, and M3 is idle 9 → 11. In the standard shop, a buffer covers these gaps.
  • The order of jobs is more important without buffers. The no-wait start delay between two jobs depends only on that pair. Thus the problem becomes a travelling-salesman problem over the jobs, and a bad order costs more.
  • Why use no-wait? Some processes do not allow a stop: hot steel, chemical reactions, food and pharmaceutical lines. There, the extra makespan is the cost of product quality.

About the example times: with J1 = [2, 3, 1], J2 = [1, 2, 4], J3 = [3, 1, 2], both versions reach Cmax = 10 with the order J2 → J1 → J3, so no-wait costs nothing there. This page changes only J2 and J3, so that the no-wait penalty is visible. The size of the penalty depends on the data.
